Part 1 · The 7 things every team must explain in the 5-minute demo (rulebook p. 12)

1What problem did you select?

One printed textbook page fails three kinds of students in the same class: a blind student can’t read it or see its diagrams, a dyslexic student (and students from Urdu- or Pashto-medium schools learning in English) gets lost in long sentences and hard words, and a deaf student misses the teacher’s spoken explanation. Teachers have no time to adapt every page three ways, so it doesn’t happen.

“Three students, one class, one printed page. Bilal is blind, Sara is dyslexic, Hamza is deaf. This page works for none of them.”
2Why does the problem matter?
  • About 1 in 6 people live with a significant disability (WHO, 2023), so this happens in every school.
  • The textbook page is the basic unit of school learning; if a student can’t use it, they fall behind in every subject.
  • It is SDG 4.5: equal access to education for persons with disabilities (also 4.1 quality education and 10.2 inclusion).
  • Simplifying text with ordinary AI is risky: it can quietly drop a fact or change a number, and a struggling student can’t tell. That is why trust is part of the problem.
3What is your proposed solution?

Roshni (روشنی, “light”): One page. Every learner. A phone photo or PDF of any textbook page becomes one lesson with:

ModeForWhat it does
Read EasyDyslexic and weaker readers, Urdu/Pashto-medium studentsSimpler text beside the original; read-aloud that highlights each sentence; hard words explained in Urdu or Pashto with audio
ListenBlind studentsSection-by-section audio, every figure described in words, full keyboard and screen-reader control

Under all of it is Fact Guard: a second AI, from a different model family, that checks every rewrite and every note so no fact is lost, changed or invented.

5How does your prototype work?
  1. Photograph or upload a page (photo or PDF, up to 3 pages, 10 MB). Gemini reads it in about 5 seconds.
  2. Choose Easier or Simplest. Qwen rewrites; Fact Guard lists the facts, audits the rewrite and repairs anything missing (up to 2 rounds). The badge shows e.g. “37/37 facts kept”.
  3. Use the modes: Read Easy, Listen, Captions (upload a lecture), Videos (paste a YouTube link).
  4. Plant 3 errors (live trust test): the app sabotages its own rewrite with a changed number, a deleted fact and an invented claim; Fact Guard catches and repairs them on screen.
  5. Practice: a voice quiz reads each question, beeps, listens, and gives a study plan that opens the exact section, word or video minute to revise.

Built with React + FastAPI; runs on a laptop or one Hugging Face Space; works offline in demo mode with prepared sample lessons.

Part 3 · Judging system and likely judge questions (rulebook p. 14)

Your EdTech & Workforce judge gives 85% of the score; the other domain judges give 15% together. So lead with learning outcomes (simpler reading, checked understanding, personalised study plan) and workforce use (accessible training material), and keep the explanation simple enough for the cross-domain judges. Judges may ask technical or non-technical questions; every teammate should be able to answer these:

“Why not just use ChatGPT?”

A chatbot rewrites text but can’t show it kept every fact, and a struggling student can’t check. Roshni has a different AI check the rewrite and quote its proof, code checks every number, and it caught 28 of 28 planted errors with zero false alarms. It also gives a blind student audio and figure descriptions, a deaf student captions, and a voice quiz.

“What if both AIs make the same mistake?”

They come from different model families, so they don’t share the same blind spots. The checker must quote the rewrite, and plain code confirms the quote exists and contains the fact’s words and numbers. Anything still uncertain stays amber with the original next to it.

“How do you know Fact Guard works?”

We sabotage our own rewrites in code (change a number, delete a fact, invent a claim), so the right answer is known in advance: 28/28 caught, and 0 of 142 false alarms when nothing changed. The script is in eval/, and the same test runs live in the app.

“Have you tested with real disabled students?”

Not yet; that’s our honest limit and our next step: a pilot with a special-education school. We designed for them (keyboard and screen-reader control, figure descriptions, voice answers, adjustable timer), but real users will teach us more.

“What does it cost, and does it scale?”

Today it runs on free tiers (PKR 0), with several keys rotating automatically and results cached so a page is processed once. A capped paid backup costs well under one US cent per task. A school or publisher would pay for higher limits; students use it free.

“What about privacy?”

No accounts and no personal data are needed. Uploaded pages and recordings are stored by content only, and a lesson can be deleted. For schools we’d add on-premise or private hosting.

“Does it work for Urdu pages or in a noisy classroom?”

Hard words are explained in Urdu and Pashto today, and the reader recognises Urdu and Pashto text, but printed Urdu pages are not yet formally evaluated. For voice answers, the app asks again when it’s unsure and never guesses; number keys always work.

“How is this different from text-to-speech tools or Immersive Reader?”

Those read the same hard text aloud. Roshni changes the page itself (simpler wording, described figures, captions, checked notes, a quiz with a study plan), for three groups at once, and proves the meaning didn’t change.

“What did you build yourselves vs. the AI assistant?”

Answer honestly and specifically: who chose the idea and design, who wrote and tested prompts, who ran the evaluations, who made the slides. The rules allow AI assistants as long as they’re disclosed, and ours is.
